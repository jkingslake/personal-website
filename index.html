I am a glaciologist at Columbia University’s Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory. My research involves modeling and observing ice and water flow in ice sheets and glaciers, combining mathematical modeling, geophysical remote sensing, and direct fieldwork.

Biography

I grew up in Reading, UK, attending Chiltern Edge Secondary School and Henley Sixth Form College. After two years working and backpacking I took a Physics BSc at the University of York, where my final-year project examined “freezing nucleation in supercooled water” under Dr. Richard Keesing’s supervision.

Between 2009 and 2013 I did a PhD in glaciology at Sheffield University under Dr. Felix Ng. My thesis, “Modeling ice-dammed lake drainage,” involved developing mathematical models to study water flow beneath glaciers.

Following my PhD I worked as a Glacier Geophysicist at the British Antarctic Survey in Cambridge (2013–2016) on a NERC-funded project led by Richard Hindmarsh, using radar and mathematical models to study West Antarctic ice flow.

I joined Columbia University as an Assistant Professor in March 2016. In June 2026 I will be moving to Imperial College London as an Associate Professor in the Department of Earth Science and Engineering.

Research Highlights

Subglacial Hydrology

During my PhD I studied the flow of water beneath glaciers by developing mathematical models. I showed that simple models can predict approximately when ice-dammed lakes will drain, that such lakes can fill and drain chaotically, and that they affect glacier flow through their impact on subglacial water pressures.

PhD student Yucheng Lu is currently investigating two-way coupling between ice flow and channelized subglacial drainage, extending these ideas to ice-sheet scale.

Glacier Geophysics

At the British Antarctic Survey I used radar to constrain present and past ice flow in the Ronne Ice Shelf region of West Antarctica. We employed a phase-sensitive radar system to measure the Raymond Effect — an ice-dynamical phenomenon at ice divides.

Subsequent work measured englacial ice flow and ice-sheet history across multiple field campaigns. Elizabeth Case used similar data to measure firn compaction, resulting in a new approach to dating ice cores. Current NSF-funded research uses radar to measure ice deformation around supraglacial lakes in Greenland.

Ice-Sheet History

In 2018 I co-led a Nature paper demonstrating that the West Antarctic Ice Sheet was smaller than today during the Holocene, in both the Weddell Sea and Ross Sea sectors. We hypothesised that subsequent readvance was caused by delayed isostatic rebound — a process sometimes called “marine ice sheet stabilization.” Evidence came from ice-penetrating radar, subglacial radiocarbon samples, and continent-wide ice-sheet modelling.

A 2016 paper used phase-sensitive radar and GPS to reconstruct three-dimensional ice flow throughout Korff Ice Rise, West Antarctica, revealing a flow-regime reorganization around 2–3 kyr ago.

Supraglacial Hydrology

I modelled the physics of supraglacial drainage and showed that melt ponds can drain stably or unstably over ice surfaces depending on pond geometry and meltwater input. This has direct implications for ice-shelf stability under future warming.

Two Nature papers (2017) demonstrated that meltwater has been moving long distances onto and across many Antarctic ice shelves for decades, challenging the assumption that Antarctic surfaces are largely dry. PhD candidate Julian Spergel’s first paper in the Journal of Glaciology quantified surface meltwater drainage and ponding on Amery Ice Shelf from 1973 to 2019.

Fieldwork

Antarctica (2013–14 and 2014–15)

During two austral summers I spent roughly two months traversing the West Antarctic Ice Sheet with a mountaineer partner, collecting GPS and ice-penetrating radar measurements of ice flow for the British Antarctic Survey. The traverses covered the Ronne Ice Shelf and surrounding ice rises.

Fieldwork was documented by photographer Iain Rudkin. A time-lapse video of the skidoo traverse from the 2013/14 austral summer was produced with music by Steve Massey.

Antarctica, 2013–15

Greenland (2017)

In April and May 2017 I spent four weeks traversing the Greenland Ice Sheet as part of an NSF-funded project investigating the refreezing of meltwater in snow and firn. The project was led by Asa Rennermalm, Regine Hock, and Marco Tedesco. Conditions on the ice sheet were memorably windy.

Greenland, 2017

Alaska (2018)

Funded by a Lenfest Junior Faculty Development Grant, Elizabeth Case and I spent two weeks on the Juneau Icefield in collaboration with the Juneau Icefield Research Program. We accessed Camp 18 by helicopter and flew a DJI drone to create 3D site models (reconstructed by Martin Pratt from the footage).

We measured vertical englacial strain rates (including firn compaction) using a phase-sensitive radar and extracted around 80 m of ice core from six locations near the ice divide. The drone also captured ogive sets produced by ice falls near the camp.

Juneau Icefield, Alaska, 2018
