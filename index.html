Biography

I grew up in Reading, UK, attending Chiltern Edge Secondary School and Henley Sixth Form College. After two years working and backpacking I took a Physics BSc at the University of York, where my final-year project examined “freezing nucleation in supercooled water” under Dr. Richard Keesing’s supervision.

Between 2009 and 2013 I did a PhD in glaciology at Sheffield University under Dr. Felix Ng. My thesis was titled “Modeling ice-dammed lake drainage” and involved developing mathematical models to study how water flows beneath glaciers.

Following my PhD I worked as a Glacier Geophysicist at the British Antarctic Survey in Cambridge (2013–2016), employed on a NERC-funded project led by Richard Hindmarsh. We used radar and mathematical models to study present-day and past ice flow in West Antarctica.

I joined Columbia University as an Assistant Professor in March 2016. In June 2026 I will be moving to Imperial College London as an Associate Professor in the Department of Earth Science and Engineering.

Teaching

My teaching at Columbia University spans glaciology, geophysical fluid dynamics, and Earth system science. I aim to bring fieldwork experiences and active research problems directly into the classroom.

Courses

Glaciology and Ice-Sheet Dynamics
Graduate — Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory / DEES

Covers the physics of glaciers and ice sheets: flow laws, mass balance, subglacial hydrology, calving, and ice-sheet response to climate forcing. Students develop mathematical models and analyse observational datasets.

Introduction to Earth and Environmental Sciences
Undergraduate — Columbia University

A broad survey of solid-Earth processes, surface dynamics, climate, and the cryosphere, with an emphasis on quantitative reasoning and real-world data.

Geophysical Fluid Dynamics
Graduate — Columbia University

Mathematical treatment of rotating, stratified flows with applications to the ocean, atmosphere, and ice sheets. Topics include shallow-water equations, wave dynamics, and instabilities.

Research Highlights

Subglacial Hydrology

During my PhD I studied the flow of water beneath glaciers by developing mathematical models. I showed that simple models can predict approximately when ice-dammed lakes will drain, that such lakes can fill and drain chaotically, and that they affect glacier flow through their impact on subglacial water pressures.

A signature visualization from this work is the Nye Attractor — a 3D plot showing the chaotic behaviour of a model glacial lake through time, named after British glaciologist John Nye (1976). More recently, PhD student Yucheng Lu is investigating two-way coupling between ice flow and channelized subglacial drainage.

Glacier Geophysics

At the British Antarctic Survey I used radar to constrain present and past ice flow in the Ronne Ice Shelf region of West Antarctica. We employed a phase-sensitive radar system to measure the Raymond Effect — a dynamical phenomenon at ice divides.

Subsequent work measured englacial ice flow and ice-sheet history across multiple field campaigns. Elizabeth Case used similar phase-sensitive radar data to measure firn compaction, resulting in a new approach to dating ice cores. Current NSF-funded research uses radar to measure ice deformation around supraglacial lakes in Greenland.

Ice-Sheet History

In 2018 I co-led a Nature paper demonstrating that the West Antarctic Ice Sheet was smaller than today during the Holocene, in both the Weddell Sea and Ross Sea sectors. We hypothesised that subsequent readvance was caused by delayed isostatic rebound following the Last Glacial Maximum — a process called “marine ice sheet stabilization.” Evidence came from ice-penetrating radar, subglacial radiocarbon samples, and continent-wide ice-sheet modelling.

A 2016 paper used phase-sensitive radar and GPS to reconstruct three-dimensional ice flow throughout the full thickness of Korff Ice Rise, West Antarctica, revealing a flow-regime reorganization around 2–3 kyr ago.

Supraglacial Hydrology

I modelled the physics of supraglacial drainage and showed that melt ponds can drain stably or unstably over ice surfaces depending on pond geometry and meltwater input. This has direct implications for ice-shelf stability under future warming.

At Lamont my group explored Antarctic supraglacial hydrology in depth. Two Nature papers (2017) demonstrated that meltwater has been moving long distances onto and across many Antarctic ice shelves for decades, challenging the assumption that Antarctic surfaces are largely dry. PhD candidate Julian Spergel’s first paper in the Journal of Glaciology quantified surface meltwater drainage and ponding on Amery Ice Shelf from 1973 to 2019, documenting a pond that inundated 55 km² over approximately 3.5 weeks.

Fieldwork

Antarctica (2013–14 and 2014–15)

During two austral summers I spent roughly two months traversing the West Antarctic Ice Sheet with a mountaineer partner, collecting GPS and ice-penetrating radar measurements of ice flow for the British Antarctic Survey. The remote traverses covered the Ronne Ice Shelf region and surrounding ice rises.

Fieldwork was documented in collaboration with photographer Iain Rudkin. A time-lapse video of the skidoo traverse from the 2013/14 austral summer was produced with music by Steve Massey.

Greenland (2017)

In April and May 2017 I spent four weeks traversing the Greenland Ice Sheet as part of an NSF-funded project investigating the refreezing of meltwater in snow and firn. The project was led by Asa Rennermalm, Regine Hock, and Marco Tedesco. Conditions on the ice sheet were memorably windy.

Alaska (2018)

Funded by a Lenfest Junior Faculty Development Grant, Elizabeth Case and I spent two weeks on the Juneau Icefield in collaboration with the Juneau Icefield Research Program, which runs a yearly six-week ski traverse of the ice field. We accessed Camp 18 by helicopter.

We measured vertical englacial strain rates (including firn compaction) using a phase-sensitive radar, and extracted around 80 m of ice core from six locations near the ice divide using a shallow coring system. Martin Pratt reconstructed a 3D model of Camp 18 from DJI drone video footage. The drone also captured spectacular ogive sets produced by ice falls near the camp.
